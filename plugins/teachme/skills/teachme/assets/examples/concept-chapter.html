What a p-value actually says

Read a p-value correctly, state what it does and does not license you to conclude, and rebuild it from scratch by simulation.

Why this concept exists at all

You run an experiment and the treatment group does better. Before you believe anything, you have to answer an awkward question: how often would a difference this large show up even if the treatment did nothing? Random assignment alone produces uneven groups. Somebody has to be taller, somebody has to recover faster. If plain randomness routinely manufactures the effect you just measured, your measurement is not evidence of anything.

The p-value is one specific answer to that question, and it is narrower than almost everyone assumes. It does not tell you whether your hypothesis is true. It does not tell you how big the effect is. It tells you one thing only, and the whole chapter rests on getting that one thing exactly right.

Definition p-value

The probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you actually got, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

Every word in that sentence is load-bearing. "At least as extreme" means you are counting a whole tail of outcomes, not just yours. "Assuming the null is true" means the entire calculation happens inside a world where the treatment does nothing — a world you do not believe in and are not testing.

Building one from nothing

The fastest way to stop mis-stating a p-value is to compute one without a formula. The logic is mechanical: simulate the null world many times, and count how often it hands you something as extreme as what you saw. That count, divided by the number of simulations, is the p-value. No distribution tables, no assumptions about normality.

Suppose you gave a new onboarding flow to 40 users and the old one to 40 users. 26 of the new group completed setup; 18 of the old group did. A difference of 8. The null world is the world where the flow made no difference at all — where those 44 completions would have happened to whichever 40 people you happened to label "new".

import random

observed = 26 - 18
pool = [1] * 44 + [0] * 36
extreme = 0

for _ in range(100_000):
    random.shuffle(pool)
    diff = sum(pool[:40]) - sum(pool[40:])
    if abs(diff) >= abs(observed):
        extreme += 1

print(extreme / 100_000)
0.0721

Record what actually happened

Eight more completions in the new group. This number never changes again — every later line is about the null world, not about your data.

Build the null world

44 completions and 36 non-completions, in one undifferentiated pool. This line is the null hypothesis, written as data: the outcomes exist, but nothing about them is attached to which flow a person saw.

Deal the outcomes at random, 100,000 times

shuffle then split 40/40. Each pass is one alternative universe in which the flow did nothing and only the coin flips differed. Nothing here consults observed — that is the point.

Count the universes at least as extreme as ours

abs() on both sides is what makes this a two-tailed test: a difference of −8 is just as surprising as +8 if you had no prior direction in mind.

Divide

0.0721. In roughly seven runs in a hundred, pure randomness produces a gap this big. That is the whole answer, and it is a statement about the null world.

🔑
The one thing to remember

The p-value is P(data this extreme │ null true). It is not P(null true │ data). Those two are different quantities, and swapping them is the single most common error in applied statistics.

Why the direction of the conditional matters

Conditional probabilities are not symmetric, and the gap between them can be enormous. Bayes' rule makes the relationship explicit:

P(H0∣D) = P(D∣H0) ⋅P(H0) P(D)
Where
H₀the null hypothesis — the treatment does nothing
Dthe data you observed, or something more extreme
P(D│H₀)the p-value — what your simulation computed
P(H₀)how plausible the null was before you ran anything
P(H₀│D)what people wrongly think a p-value is

The quantity everyone actually wants is on the left. The quantity a p-value gives you is one factor on the right. Getting from one to the other requires P(H₀), a prior belief that the p-value framework deliberately refuses to supply. That refusal is not an oversight — it is the whole design choice, and it is why a p-value can never, on its own, tell you the probability that your hypothesis is true.

P(H₀ ∧ D) = P(D │ H₀) · P(H₀)
Definition of conditional probability, written for the joint event.
P(H₀ ∧ D) = P(H₀ │ D) · P(D)
The same joint event, conditioned the other way round.
P(H₀ │ D) · P(D) = P(D │ H₀) · P(H₀)
Both expressions describe one quantity, so they are equal.
P(H₀ │ D) = P(D │ H₀) · P(H₀) / P(D)
Divide through by P(D). The two conditionals differ by exactly the factor P(H₀)/P(D).

The 0.05 threshold, and what it is not

0.05 is a convention, chosen for convenience by Ronald Fisher in the 1920s and never derived from anything. Nothing physical changes between 0.049 and 0.051. Treating the threshold as a boundary between "real" and "not real" turns a continuous measure of surprise into a binary verdict it cannot support.

✕ What people say

"p = 0.03, so the treatment works."

"p = 0.08, so there is no effect."

"p = 0.001, so the effect is large."

✓ What is supportable

"Data this extreme arises 3% of the time under the null."

"This study could not distinguish the effect from noise."

"The effect is precisely estimated — its size is a separate number."

The third row is the one that catches careful people. A very small p-value means the result would be very unusual under the null; with a large enough sample, a trivially small effect produces a tiny p-value. Significance and importance are unrelated quantities, and only the effect size and its confidence interval speak to the second.

What you want to knowWhat answers it
Could this plausibly be noise?p-value
How big is the effect?Effect size (difference in means, odds ratio, d)
How precisely do we know it?Confidence interval
How likely is my hypothesis?Nothing frequentist — you need a prior and Bayes

The three misreadings, with what to say instead

🚫
"p = 0.04 means there is a 4% chance the null is true"

This is the inverted conditional. It requires P(H₀), which no p-value contains. Say instead: "if the null were true, data this extreme would arise 4% of the time."

🚫
"p = 0.20 means the treatment has no effect"

Failing to reject is not evidence of absence — an underpowered study fails to reject almost regardless of the truth. Say instead: "this study was not able to detect an effect of the size we could realistically expect," and report the interval.

🚫
"we tested 20 outcomes and one came back at p = 0.03"

At a 0.05 threshold, one in twenty null tests clears it by chance alone. Twenty tests make one "finding" the expected outcome, not a surprise. Pre-register which outcome is primary, or correct for the number of comparisons.

✎
Exercise · Break the threshold on purpose

Take the simulation above and change one thing: make the observed difference 6 instead of 8, keeping the pool identical. Before running it, predict whether the p-value roughly doubles, roughly halves, or moves a little. Then run it and explain the result in one sentence that does not contain the word "significant".

Solution

Set observed = 6. The p-value rises to roughly 0.20 — it more than doubles, because you are now counting a much wider tail of the null distribution.

The sentence: "a gap of six arises about one time in five when the flow makes no difference, so this experiment cannot separate the flow from chance."

Notice what did not happen: nothing about the world changed between a gap of eight and a gap of six. The p-value moved smoothly, the way a continuous measure should. Any procedure that reports the first as a discovery and the second as a null result is inventing a distinction the data does not contain.

Recap

  • A p-value is P(data at least this extreme │ null true) — a statement about the null world, not about your hypothesis.
  • You can compute one by shuffling, with no formula and no distributional assumption.
  • P(H₀│D) and P(D│H₀) differ by the factor P(H₀)/P(D); the p-value supplies only the second.
  • 0.05 is a convention. 0.049 and 0.051 are the same finding.
  • Significance answers "could this be noise?" — never "how big?" or "how likely is my hypothesis?"
Checkpoint

A trial reports p = 0.01. Which statement is supportable?

The second. The first and third invert the conditional — they are claims about P(H₀│D), which needs a prior the p-value does not contain. The fourth confuses significance with magnitude: with a large sample, a tiny effect produces a tiny p-value.

A study of 900 people finds p = 0.002 for a 0.3% improvement in completion rate. What should you conclude?

The third. A small p-value with a large sample says the effect is distinguishable from zero — nothing more. Whether 0.3% is worth anything is a decision about cost and value, which no statistical test can make for you.

Across 20 independent tests of true null hypotheses, what fraction do you expect to clear a 0.05 threshold by chance alone?

0.05 — one test in twenty. That is what the threshold means: it is the long-run rate of false positives you have agreed to tolerate. Running twenty tests and reporting the one that cleared it converts a guaranteed artefact into a headline.
