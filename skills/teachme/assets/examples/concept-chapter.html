What a p-value actually says

Read a p-value correctly, state what it does and does not license you to conclude, and rebuild it from scratch by simulation.

IntermediateCore
After this chapter you can
  • State in one sentence what a p-value is the probability of — and what it is not
  • Compute one by simulation, without any distributional formula
  • Explain why p = 0.04 and p = 0.06 are not two different findings

You run an experiment and the treatment group does better. Before you believe it, ask: how often would a difference this large show up even if the treatment did nothing? Random assignment alone produces uneven groups, so if chance routinely manufactures the effect you measured, you have measured nothing.

The p-value is one specific answer to that question, and it is narrower than almost everyone assumes: not whether your hypothesis is true, not how big the effect is.

Definition p-value

The probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you actually got, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

"At least as extreme" means you count a whole tail of outcomes, not just yours. "Assuming the null is true" means the calculation happens inside a world where the treatment does nothing — one you do not believe in and are not testing.

Building one from nothing

The fastest way to stop mis-stating a p-value is to compute one without a formula: simulate the null world many times and count how often it hands you something as extreme as what you saw.

Say a new onboarding flow went to 40 users and the old one to 40. 26 of the new group completed setup, 18 of the old — a gap of 8. In the null world the flow changed nothing: those 44 completions would have happened to whichever 40 people you labelled "new".

import random

observed = 26 - 18
pool = [1] * 44 + [0] * 36
extreme = 0

for _ in range(100_000):
    random.shuffle(pool)
    diff = sum(pool[:40]) - sum(pool[40:])
    if abs(diff) >= abs(observed):
        extreme += 1

print(extreme / 100_000)
0.0721

Record what actually happened

Eight more completions in the new group. Every later line is about the null world, not your data.

Build the null world

44 completions and 36 non-completions in one pool. This line is the null hypothesis, written as data: the outcomes exist, but none is tied to which flow a person saw.

Deal the outcomes at random, 100,000 times

shuffle, then split 40/40. Each pass is one universe where the flow did nothing and only chance differed. Nothing here consults observed.

Count the universes at least as extreme as ours

abs() makes this two-tailed: −8 is as surprising as +8 when you had no direction in mind.

Divide

0.0721. About seven runs in a hundred, pure chance produces a gap this big.

🔑
The one thing to remember

The p-value is P(data this extreme │ null true). It is not P(null true │ data). Those two are different quantities, and swapping them is the single most common error in applied statistics.

Why the direction of the conditional matters

Conditional probabilities are not symmetric. Bayes' rule shows the gap:

P(H0∣D) = P(D∣H0) ⋅P(H0) P(D)
Where
H₀the null hypothesis — the treatment does nothing
Dthe data you observed, or something more extreme
P(D│H₀)the p-value — what your simulation computed
P(H₀)how plausible the null was before you ran anything
P(H₀│D)what people wrongly think a p-value is

What everyone wants is on the left; a p-value is one factor on the right. Bridging them needs P(H₀), a prior the framework deliberately refuses to supply — which is why a p-value alone can never give the probability that your hypothesis is true.

What 0.05 is not

0.05 is a convention Ronald Fisher picked for convenience in the 1920s. Nothing physical changes between 0.049 and 0.051, so treating it as the line between "real" and "not real" turns a continuous measure of surprise into a verdict it cannot support.

✕ What people say

"p = 0.03, so the treatment works."

"p = 0.08, so there is no effect."

"p = 0.001, so the effect is large."

✓ What is supportable

"Data this extreme arises 3% of the time under the null."

"This study could not tell the effect from noise."

"The effect is precisely estimated — its size is a separate number."

The last row catches careful people: with a large enough sample a trivial effect gets a tiny p-value. Only effect size and its confidence interval speak to importance.

🚫
"p = 0.04 means there is a 4% chance the null is true"

The inverted conditional again — it needs P(H₀), which no p-value contains. Say instead: "if the null were true, data this extreme would arise 4% of the time."

✎
Exercise · Break the threshold on purpose

Change the simulation so the observed difference is 6, not 8, with the same pool. Predict whether the p-value roughly doubles, halves, or barely moves — then run it and explain the result in one sentence without the word "significant".

Solution

Set observed = 6. The p-value rises to roughly 0.20 — it more than doubles, because you now count a much wider tail.

"A gap of six arises about one time in five when the flow makes no difference, so this experiment cannot separate the flow from chance." Nothing about the world changed between eight and six; the p-value moved smoothly, as a continuous measure should.

In short

  • A p-value is P(data at least this extreme │ null true) — a statement about the null world, not about your hypothesis.
  • You can compute one by shuffling, with no formula and no distributional assumption.
  • 0.05 is a convention, and significance never answers "how big?" or "how likely is my hypothesis?"
Checkpoint

A trial reports p = 0.01. Which statement is supportable?

A p-value describes the null world. It is a statement about how surprising the data are if nothing is going on, never about how likely your hypothesis is.

The onboarding test gave 26 vs 18 completions out of 40 per group and simulated p ≈ 0.07. You rerun the same completion rates with 400 users per group. What happens to the simulated p-value?

The same gap is harder for chance to produce with more people. A 20-point gap over 400 per group would almost never appear in the shuffled pool, so p collapses. That is why a p-value alone can call a trivial effect "significant".

An A/B test on 1.2 million users finds p = 0.001 for a 0.3-point rise in completion rate. What do you conclude?

Real but maybe not worth acting on. 0.3 points sits over three standard errors from zero — distinguishable, nothing more. Whether it is worth shipping is a cost-and-value decision no test can make.

Across 20 independent tests of true null hypotheses, what fraction do you expect to clear a 0.05 threshold by chance alone?

0.05 — one test in twenty. That is the false-positive rate the threshold agrees to tolerate; run twenty tests and report the one that cleared it, and you have manufactured a headline.
